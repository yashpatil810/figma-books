Heading2	Introduction
P	Electric charge can move from one body to another under suitable conditions. However, all materials do not allow the movement of electric charges with equal ease. Based on their ability to permit the flow of electric charge, materials are broadly classified into:
P•	Conductors
P•	Insulators
P	This classification forms the basis of electrical technology and circuit design.
Heading2	Conductors
Heading3	Definition
P	Materials that allow electric charges to flow through them easily are called conductors.
P	In conductors, some electrons are loosely bound to atoms and can move freely within the material. These free electrons are responsible for electrical conduction.
Heading2	Scientific Principle
P	Metals contain a large number of free electrons. When a potential difference is applied across a conductor, these electrons move in a particular direction, resulting in the flow of electric current.
P	Thus, electrical conduction in conductors occurs due to the movement of free electrons.
Heading2	Examples of Conductors
Heading3	Metals
P•	Copper
P•	Aluminium
P•	Silver
P•	Gold
P•	Iron
Heading3	Other Conducting Materials
P•	Graphite
P•	Human body
P•	Tap water
P•	Acidic solutions
P•	Salt solutions
P	Among common metals, silver is the best conductor of electricity, although copper is more widely used because of its lower cost and high conductivity.
Heading2	Applications of Conductors
Heading3	Electrical Wiring
P	Copper and aluminium are used in household wiring because they allow current to pass easily with low energy loss.
Heading3	Electrical Appliances
P	Internal components of electrical devices are generally made of conducting materials.
Heading3	Earthing
P	The metallic body of electrical appliances is connected to earth using conducting wires to ensure safety.
Heading3	Transmission Lines
P	Aluminium cables reinforced with steel are commonly used for long-distance transmission of electricity.
Heading2	Insulators
Heading3	Definition
P	Materials that do not allow electric charges to flow through them easily are called insulators.
P	Insulators do not contain free electrons in sufficient quantity. Therefore, charges cannot move freely through them.
Heading2	Scientific Principle
P	In insulators, electrons are tightly bound to their atoms. Since these electrons cannot move freely, electric current cannot pass through the material under ordinary conditions.
Heading2	Examples of Insulators
P•	Rubber
P•	Plastic
P•	Glass
P•	Wood
P•	Dry air
P•	Porcelain
P•	Mica
P•	Ebonite
Heading2	Applications of Insulators
Heading3	Covering of Electrical Wires
P	Electric wires are covered with plastic or rubber to prevent electric shocks.
Heading3	Switches and Plug Tops
P	The outer bodies of switches and plugs are made of insulating materials for user safety.
Heading3	Insulating Supports
P	Transmission wires are mounted on porcelain insulators to prevent leakage of current to the ground.
Heading3	Electrical Tools
P	Handles of screwdrivers, pliers, and testers are made of insulating materials.

Heading3	Poor Conductors

Heading3	Pure Water
P	Pure or distilled water is a poor conductor of electricity because it contains very few ions.
Heading3	Tap Water
P	Tap water contains dissolved salts and minerals that produce ions, making it a conductor of electricity.

P•	Metals are generally good conductors.
P•	Most non-metals are insulators.
P•	Graphite is an exception among non-metals because it conducts electricity.
P•	Conductivity depends upon the availability of free electrons or ions.

P•	Atomic structure of the material
P•	Temperature of the material
P	In metals:
P•	Resistance generally increases with temperature.
P	In some non-metals:
P•	Conductivity may increase with temperature.

P	Lightning and Lightning Conductor
P	Lightning – A Large-Scale Electrical Discharge in the Atmosphere
P	[IMG]
P	Lightning is a natural electrical phenomenon produced by the accumulation and sudden discharge of electric charges inside clouds or between clouds and the Earth. It is one of the largest natural examples of static electricity.
P	During thunderstorms, strong air currents continuously move water droplets, ice crystals, and hail particles inside clouds. Frequent collisions and friction between these particles produce electric charges.
P	As a result:• The upper part of the cloud generally becomes positively charged.• The lower part becomes negatively charged.• Sometimes a small positively charged region forms near the bottom of the cloud.
P	The negatively charged lower region of the cloud induces positive charges on the Earth’s surface directly below it due to electrical induction.
P	Normally, air acts as an insulator and prevents the movement of charges. However, when the amount of charge becomes extremely large, the potential difference between oppositely charged regions increases enormously. At this stage, the insulating property of air breaks down. The air becomes ionised and suddenly allows electric current to pass through it.
P	This sudden and rapid discharge of electricity produces a bright flash called lightning.
P	The electric discharge heats the surrounding air to extremely high temperatures, sometimes close to 30,000°C. The heated air expands very rapidly and produces shock waves. These shock waves are heard as thunder.
P	Types of Lightning
P•	Intra-cloud LightningElectrical discharge occurring within the same cloud.
P•	Cloud-to-Cloud LightningElectrical discharge occurring between two different clouds.
P•	Cloud-to-Ground LightningElectrical discharge occurring between a cloud and the Earth. This type can cause serious damage to life and property.
P	Why Thunder is Heard After Lightning
P	Light travels much faster than sound.
P	• Speed of light = 3 × 10⁸ m/s• Speed of sound in air ≈ 340 m/s
P	Therefore, the flash of lightning is seen almost immediately, while the sound of thunder reaches us after a short delay.
P	Estimating the Distance of Lightning
P	The approximate distance of lightning from an observer can be estimated using the time interval between the flash and the thunder.
P	Distance = Speed of sound × Time interval
P	Example:
P	If thunder is heard 4 seconds after the flash,
P	Distance = 340 × 4= 1360 m
P	Thus, the lightning occurred approximately 1.36 km away.
P	Safety Precautions During Lightning
P	• Avoid standing under isolated trees during thunderstorms.• Stay away from open fields and water bodies.• Do not touch electrical appliances or metal objects during severe lightning storms.• Remain inside a building or a closed vehicle whenever possible.
P	Scientific Significance of Lightning
P	• Lightning helps convert atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogen compounds useful for plants.• The study of lightning contributed significantly to the development of electrical science.• Atmospheric electrical discharges help scientists understand charge separation and high-voltage phenomena.
P	Concept Insight
P	Lightning is essentially a giant electric spark produced when accumulated charges discharge through ionised air. The same principles of charging by friction, induction, attraction, and repulsion observed in laboratory experiments operate in thunderstorms on a much larger scale.
P	Did You Know?
P	A single lightning strike may involve millions of volts of potential difference and currents of several thousand amperes.
P	Lightning Conductor
P	[IMG]
P	A lightning conductor is a protective device used to safeguard buildings and tall structures from the damaging effects of lightning. It was developed by Benjamin Franklin after he demonstrated that lightning is an electrical discharge.
P	A lightning conductor consists of:• A long, thick metal rod, usually made of copper or aluminium• A pointed upper end fixed at the highest point of the building• A thick metal strip connected to the rod and extending down the side of the building• A metal plate buried deep inside the ground
P	The metal rod and strip provide a low-resistance conducting path between the atmosphere and the Earth.
P	Working Principle of a Lightning Conductor
P	During a thunderstorm, a large potential difference develops between the charged clouds and the Earth. If lightning strikes the building, the electric charge passes through the metal conductor directly into the ground instead of flowing through the building structure.
P	Thus, the lightning conductor safely transfers the enormous electric charge to the Earth and protects the building from:• Fire hazards• Structural damage• Electrical damage• Risk to human life
P	The pointed upper end of the conductor helps the charges leak gradually into the air, reducing the possibility of a violent discharge.
P	ApplicationsLightning conductors are installed on:• Tall buildings• Towers• Factories• Monuments• Airports• Power stations
P	Safety Precautions During Thunderstorms• Do not stand under isolated tall trees.• Avoid open fields during lightning storms.• Stay away from metal poles, electric wires, and water bodies.• Avoid using wired electrical appliances during severe thunderstorms.
P	Scientific Principle
P	A lightning conductor works on the principles of:• Electrical conduction• Earthing• Electric discharge through a low-resistance path
P	Since metals are good conductors of electricity, the charge prefers to flow through the conductor rather than through the building materials.
P	Concept Insight
P	Lightning always follows the path of least electrical resistance. A lightning conductor provides a safe conducting path from the cloud to the Earth, thereby preventing damage to structures.
P	Did You Know?
P	Benjamin Franklin demonstrated the electrical nature of lightning through his famous kite experiment in 1752. His work laid the foundation for the invention of the lightning conductor.

P	29. Distinguish between conductors and insulators. Give two examples of each.
P	Answer: Conductors are materials that allow electric charges to flow through them easily (e.g., copper, aluminium). Insulators are materials that do not allow electric charges to pass through them (e.g., wood, plastic). Conductors are used for making wires, while insulators are used for covering wires to prevent shocks.
P	30. What is lightning? How does a lightning conductor protect a building from damage?
P	Answer: Lightning is a huge electric spark caused by the sudden discharge of a large amount of electric charge accumulated in the clouds. A lightning conductor is a thick metal strip with a sharp pointed top fixed on buildings. It provides an easy path for the charge to flow safely into the ground, protecting the building from damage.
P	31. Explain the working of an electric fuse. Why is it called a safety device?
P	Answer: An electric fuse contains a thin wire of low melting point. When current exceeds the safe limit, the wire gets heated, melts, and breaks the circuit. This stops the flow of excess current and protects the appliance and wiring from damage. It is called a safety device because it prevents fire and damage due to overloading or short circuit.
P	Long Answer Type Questions / Essay Type Questions
P	32. (Diagram: Show electromagnet, soft iron strip with hammer, gong, contact screw and spring.)
P	When the switch is pressed, current flows through the coil and the electromagnet attracts the soft iron strip. The hammer strikes the gong and produces sound. At the same time, the strip loses contact with the screw, breaking the circuit. The electromagnet loses its magnetism and the strip returns to its original position due to the spring. This again completes the circuit. The process repeats rapidly, so the bell keeps ringing continuously as long as the switch is pressed.
P	33. In power plants, electricity is generated by converting mechanical energy into electrical energy using an electric generator. Fast-flowing water (from dams) or high-pressure steam rotates a turbine at high speed. The turbine is connected to a dynamo. As the dynamo rotates, it converts mechanical energy into electrical energy.
P	This electricity is stepped up to high voltage and transmitted through thick cables and towers to cities and villages. Near our homes, it is stepped down by transformers and supplied through live and neutral wires.
P	34. Overloading occurs when too many appliances are connected to a single socket and draw more current than the circuit can handle. Short circuiting occurs when the live and neutral wires touch each other due to damaged insulation, causing a sudden huge flow of current.
P	Both a fuse and MCB protect the circuit. When current exceeds the safe limit, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit, while the MCB automatically switches off, stopping the dangerous current and preventing fire or damage to appliances.
P	35. When electric current passes through a wire, electrical energy is converted into heat energy. This is called the heating effect of electric current.
P	Filament bulbs waste more electricity because most of the energy is converted into heat instead of light. CFLs and LED bulbs produce very little heat and give more light, so they save electricity.
P	Two ways to reduce wastage at home: (i) Switch off lights, fans and appliances when not in use, (ii) Replace filament bulbs with CFLs or LED bulbs.

P	69. Charging by conduction requires direct contact between a charged and an uncharged body. Charging by induction takes place without any contact — only by bringing a charged body near an uncharged body.
P	70. Metals are good conductors because they have free electrons that can move easily. Plastic and wood are good insulators because they do not have free electrons, so charges cannot flow through them.
P	71. The sharp pointed top of a lightning conductor allows electric charge to leak gradually into the air, reducing the chance of a sudden heavy lightning strike on the building.
P	72. A fuse is a thin wire of low melting point connected in series. When current exceeds the safe limit, the wire heats up, melts and breaks the circuit. It is a safety device because it protects appliances and wiring from damage due to overloading or short circuit.
